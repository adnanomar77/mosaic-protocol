\begin{abstract}
Distributed ledgers commonly make a globally ordered block or log the primary integrity boundary, even when application transitions touch disjoint resources. This paper introduces \emph{Evidence-Complete Transition Closure} (ECTC), a protocol semantics in which a state transition is not terminal merely because a validator accepted it. A Capsule must either reach a Closed outcome carrying a ClosureProof and successor StateSeal, a Conflict outcome carrying independently verifiable ConflictEvidence, or an Abandoned outcome carrying an AbandonProof. The protocol therefore binds the predecessor state, transition claim, witness obligation, and successor state, while preserving incompatible and abandoned claims as auditable artifacts. MOSAIC (Mutual-Obligation State And Integrity Capsules) is an executable prototype of this lifecycle. It includes Ed25519 signatures, leaderless TCP dissemination, SQLite WAL recovery, weighted membership and settlement, commit--reveal randomness, Reed--Solomon availability, deterministic execution, onboarding, and a hash-chained event log, but these components are treated as implementation support rather than individual novelty claims. We state an unweighted quorum-intersection argument for closure exclusivity, describe a weighted extension, map eight paper invariants to a bounded TLA+ model, and add adversarial state-machine tests. In a 1,000-operation serialized local workload, disjoint transitions produced 3.54/3.63 ms p50/p95 latency, while a genuine same-resource competing-claims workload produced 4.19/5.02 ms p50/p95 latency, rejected 1,000 losing claims, and generated 1,000 ConflictEvidence artifacts. Batch sizes of 10 and 100 reduced measured protocol bytes per successful operation to 2,530.78 and 2,473.96 bytes. A seven-process local long-run with two Byzantine test identities, a kill/restart event, and partial-frame probes completed all 120 operations with liveness 1.0, zero unexpected operational errors, p50 36.80 ms, and p95 233.56 ms. These are \texttt{LOCAL\_EMULATION} observations, not WAN or public-testnet evidence. The contribution is a reproducible protocol-semantics hypothesis and systems artifact, not a claim of universal blockchain superiority.

\section{Introduction}
Blockchains are often introduced through the abstraction of a chain of blocks \cite{Nakamoto2008}. The engineering problem is more specific: independent machines must agree on which state transitions are valid, how competing claims are handled, what state follows an accepted transition, and how a later observer can verify both accepted and rejected claims. A global order is a powerful general mechanism, but it also places unrelated activity through common ordering and commit paths. Recent systems have therefore decomposed dissemination, ordering, execution, and certification in different ways. HotStuff provides a partially synchronous Byzantine fault-tolerant replication protocol with a formal safety argument and responsive communication phases \cite{Yin2019HotStuff}; Narwhal and Tusk separate reliable dissemination from BFT ordering \cite{Danezis2022Narwhal}; FastPay uses Byzantine consistent broadcast for a restricted pre-funded settlement workload \cite{Baudet2020FastPay}; and Sui Lutris combines a consensusless path with consensus when concurrent access patterns create an ordering risk \cite{Blackshear2024SuiLutris}. These systems establish that a block is not the only possible abstraction around which a ledger can organize agreement.

MOSAIC studies a narrower question: can the \emph{state transition itself} be made the integrity boundary, with a verifiable terminal artifact for every observed outcome? Its basic object, a Capsule, names a predecessor StateSeal, a deterministic operation, an attempt and epoch context, and witness obligations. A validator may issue a signed WitnessReceipt, but a receipt alone does not mutate state. A first-claim lock prevents a validator from acknowledging incompatible claims for the same resource and protocol context. A ClosureProof binds compatible receipts to the Capsule and its predecessor. Deterministic execution computes a successor root, which becomes a StateSeal. If a competing claim is observed, ConflictEvidence records it; if a claim cannot close under its obligations, an AbandonProof records that outcome. The proposed semantics are called Evidence-Complete Transition Closure (ECTC).

ECTC is intentionally not defined by the use of Ed25519, SQLite, Reed--Solomon coding, gossip, quorum voting, deterministic execution, commit--reveal randomness, or stake settlement. Those are known components. The proposed contribution is the lifecycle and its integrity semantics: a transition is not silently lost between acceptance and application, and a negative outcome is represented by evidence rather than by an unexplained absence. This claim is adjacent to object-centric execution, optimistic concurrency, authenticated state, and BFT certificates. Tango studied distributed data structures over a shared log \cite{Balakrishnan2013Tango}; Block-STM studied deterministic optimistic parallel execution and dependency validation \cite{Gelashvili2022BlockSTM}; and process-commutative distributed objects studied the space between BFT CRDTs and totally ordered ledgers \cite{Frey2024BFTCRDT}. MOSAIC therefore does not claim to be the first object-centric, conflict-aware, or selectively ordered ledger.

The paper makes three contributions. First, it defines ECTC as a protocol abstraction and gives executable representations for Capsule, WitnessReceipt, first-claim lock, ClosureProof, StateSeal, ConflictEvidence, and AbandonProof. Second, it gives eight protocol invariants and a quorum-intersection argument for closure exclusivity, while connecting the argument to a bounded TLA+ model and explicitly stating what remains only modeled, implemented, or observed. Third, it provides a reproducible implementation and evaluation: an adversarial state-machine matrix, a 1,000-operation disjoint-versus-contended workload, a batching study, a 4/7/10/16-validator local scaling sweep, a detailed byte decomposition, and a seven-process restart rehearsal.

The paper is organized as follows. Section 2 defines the system model and problem. Section 3 presents the MOSAIC protocol. Section 4 defines ECTC, invariants, quorum assumptions, and closure exclusivity. Section 5 describes the implementation. Section 6 reports formal and adversarial validation. Section 7 evaluates workloads, scaling, batching, recovery, and communication overhead. Section 8 discusses related work. Section 9 analyzes security and limitations. Sections 10--12 cover reproducibility, discussion, and conclusion.

\section{System Model and Problem Definition}
Let $R$ be a set of logical resources, such as an account, object, contract slot, or application-specific state domain. Each resource $r\in R$ has a current state root and a StateSeal identifying the state that an honest implementation believes to be current. A protocol context is the pair $(r,e)$ consisting of a resource and an epoch. A transition may be independent of another transition when their resource contexts are disjoint; this does not mean that MOSAIC eliminates ordering, but that it makes the scope of ordering explicit at the resource boundary rather than assuming that every transition requires the same global ordering path.

The validator set is $V$. Model A is unweighted: $N=|V|$, at most $f$ validators are Byzantine, $N\geq 3f+1$, and a closure quorum has at least $2f+1$ validators. Model B is weighted: every validator $v$ has positive weight $w(v)$, total weight is $W$, Byzantine weight is at most $W/3$, and a quorum has weight strictly greater than $2W/3$. The implementation supports weighted thresholds; the principal theorem is stated first for Model A so that the assumptions are not hidden inside a single overloaded definition.

The adversary may submit malformed, replayed, stale, conflicting, or delayed messages; may cause a validator to fail and restart; may withhold a beacon reveal; and may cause shard loss in the local availability layer. The model does not assume protection against compromised private keys, a compromised host operating system, supply-chain compromise, an economically dominant stake coalition, or network partitions beyond the declared BFT assumptions. The evaluation uses deterministic local adversarial schedules and does not substitute one host for independent WAN operators.

The problem is to make the following four relations explicit and auditable:
\begin{enumerate}
\item a transition claim must bind to a known predecessor state;
\item witness obligations must be bound to the same claim and protocol context;
\item a successful transition must bind the closure certificate to the successor state;
\item a competing or abandoned claim must have a terminal evidence artifact rather than disappearing from the observable protocol history.
\end{enumerate}

\section{MOSAIC Protocol}
\subsection{Core objects}
A Capsule is modeled as
\begin{equation}
 C=(r,p,o,n,w,e,\sigma),
\end{equation}
where $r$ is the resource identifier, $p$ the predecessor StateSeal identifier, $o$ the deterministic operation, $n$ the attempt/nonce context, $w$ the witness rule, $e$ the protocol epoch, and $\sigma$ the originator signature. The Capsule is a signed claim that a transition may be attempted from $p$; it is not itself a state mutation.

A WitnessReceipt is a signed statement by validator $v$ about a Capsule. It binds the Capsule identifier, predecessor identifier, witness identifier, epoch, attempt, status, and signature. An ACCEPT receipt is eligible for closure only when its signature and context verify and the validator has not already locked an incompatible claim. An ABANDON receipt contributes to an AbandonProof when the abandonment rules permit it.

A first-claim lock $L(r,v)$ is initially empty. A validator may set the lock to one Capsule for the resource context. Repeated observations of the same Capsule and attempt are idempotent; an incompatible claim produces ConflictEvidence. The lock is local state, but a quorum of compatible receipts makes the local non-equivocation assumption relevant to closure safety.

A ClosureProof contains one Capsule identifier, its predecessor, epoch and attempt, the ordered set of compatible WitnessReceipts, the signer identifiers, and a proof identifier. Closure is accepted only if the proof verifies, its quorum threshold is met, and no incompatible evidence for the same predecessor has been recorded. A StateSeal binds the successor state root, resource, version, owner/capability context, and transition digest. Applying a ClosureProof computes and persists the successor StateSeal atomically with the implementation's state transition.

ConflictEvidence contains two distinct Capsule identifiers sharing a predecessor and their originator signatures. AbandonProof contains the Capsule, predecessor, epoch, attempt, signer identifiers and ABANDON receipts. BundleClosure composes closures for independent resources and applies them at one visibility boundary; it is auxiliary to the ECTC contribution.

\subsection{Closure algorithm}
The following pseudocode states the normal lifecycle without conflating receipt, closure, execution, and persistence.

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Capsule Closure Algorithm.},label={alg:closure}]
Input: Capsule C, predecessor seal P
1. Verify signature(C), epoch(C), nonce(C), and predecessor(P).
2. If P is not restored, enqueue C as pending; do not classify it as daemon failure.
3. Acquire first-claim lock L(resource(C), validator).
4. If an incompatible lock exists, emit ConflictEvidence(C, incumbent).
5. Collect valid ACCEPT WitnessReceipts for C.
6. If quorum Q is not satisfied, retain C as pending or create AbandonProof.
7. Construct and verify ClosureProof(C, Q).
8. Execute C deterministically from P.
9. Compute successor root S' and construct StateSeal(S').
10. Persist closure, successor seal, and audit event atomically.
11. Return Closed(C, ClosureProof, StateSeal).
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Conflict handling.},label={alg:conflict}]
Input: Capsule C and incumbent claim I for the same predecessor
1. Verify that C and I are distinct and share the same lock context.
2. Preserve both claim identifiers and originator signatures.
3. Construct ConflictEvidence(C, I).
4. Reject a ClosureProof that omits the conflict or tries to close both claims.
5. Return Conflict(C, ConflictEvidence).
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Recovery handling.},label={alg:recovery}]
Input: incoming Capsule C and durable store W
1. Restore known and current StateSeals, capsules, receipts, and closures from W.
2. If predecessor(C) is absent, place C in a bounded pending queue.
3. When predecessor(C) becomes known, re-run validation and receipt processing.
4. If a valid closure and successor seal are restored, expose Closed(C).
5. Otherwise preserve ConflictEvidence or AbandonProof if present.
6. Append a hash-chained recovery event.
\end{lstlisting}

\section{Evidence-Complete Transition Closure}
\subsection{Definition and evidence predicate}
Let $C$ be a Capsule and let $\Pi$ be a candidate ClosureProof. Write $U(\Pi)$ for the receipt set after deduplication by witness identifier, $I(\Pi)=\{r.\mathit{witness\_id}:r\in U(\Pi)\}$, $M$ for the current validator membership, $w(v)$ for member weight, and $\theta$ for the configured closure threshold. In Model A, $w(v)=1$ and $\theta=2f+1$. The paper uses the following implementation-grounded predicate:
\begin{align*}
\mathsf{VCP}(C,\Pi)\;\Longleftrightarrow\;&\Pi.\mathit{capsule\_id}=C.\mathit{capsule\_id}\ \land\ \Pi.\mathit{predecessor\_id}=C.\mathit{predecessor\_id}\\
&\land\ \Pi.\mathit{epoch}=C.\mathit{epoch}\ \land\ \Pi.\mathit{attempt}=C.\mathit{attempt}\\
&\land\ I(\Pi)=\operatorname{set}(\Pi.\mathit{signer\_ids})\land |I(\Pi)|=|\Pi.\mathit{signer\_ids}|\\
&\land\ \forall r\in U(\Pi):\mathsf{VerifyReceipt}(r)\land r.\mathit{status}=\texttt{ACCEPT}\land r.\mathit{capsule\_id}=C.\mathit{capsule\_id}\\
&\land\ \sum_{v\in I(\Pi)}w(v)\geq\theta\\
&\land\ \Pi.\mathit{proof\_id}=\mathsf{ClosureDigest}(C,U(\Pi)).
\end{align*}
where
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathsf{ClosureDigest}(C,U)=H\!\bigl(&\texttt{MOSAIC/CLOSURE/v0.1},C.\mathit{capsule\_id},C.\mathit{predecessor\_id},\\
& C.\mathit{epoch},C.\mathit{attempt},\operatorname{sort}\{r.\mathit{receipt\_id}:r\in U\}\bigr).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Here $\mathsf{VerifyReceipt}$ includes membership lookup, signature verification, predecessor/epoch/attempt binding, and the current protocol epoch. A proof is therefore not valid merely because it contains enough signer names: it must be a canonical, identifier-bound set of individually valid ACCEPT receipts whose recomputed digest and weighted threshold agree with the proof. A closure is admissible for registration only when no previously recorded \texttt{ConflictEvidence} has the same predecessor identifier; this is the implementation-wide guard enforced by \texttt{register\_closure}. The theorem's incompatibility domain is the narrower same-resource, same-epoch, same-attempt first-claim context.

For a local observation at time $t$, define
\begin{equation}
\mathsf{Closed}_t(C)\Longleftrightarrow\exists\Pi,S:\mathsf{VCP}(C,\Pi)\land\mathsf{Registered}(C,\Pi)\land\mathsf{SuccessorSeal}(C,S).
\end{equation}
The other terminal outcomes require, respectively, a verifiable \texttt{ConflictEvidence} naming $C$ and its incompatible claim, or a verifiable \texttt{AbandonProof} naming $C$. We call the observation \emph{evidence-complete} exactly when one of these three terminal predicates is true. If none is true, $Outcome_t(C)=\mathsf{Pending}$; the protocol must not silently infer success, failure, or eventual completion.

The implementation exposes this definition through the \texttt{ECTCStatus} and \texttt{TransitionOutcome} types. \texttt{MosaicProtocol.transition\_outcome} returns Closed only after both a stored closure and a matching successor seal are locally observed, returns Conflict when evidence names the Capsule, returns Abandoned when the abandonment proof exists, and otherwise returns \texttt{None}. Thus ECTC specifies the validity and classification of an observed terminal artifact; it does not assert that such an artifact will eventually be produced or observed by every participant.

\subsection{Protocol invariants}
The paper invariants are stated below. The TLA+ file \texttt{formal/mosaic\_safety.tla} uses the same names; the bounded model maps finite-state transitions to these properties.
\begin{align*}
I1\ &: Apply(C)\Rightarrow ValidClosure(C),\\
I2\ &: Closure(C)\Rightarrow pred(C)=currentState,\\
I3\ &: StateSeal(C)\Rightarrow H(C,p,s',Q)=Seal,\\
I4\ &: Closed(C_1,r)\land Closed(C_2,r)\Rightarrow Compatible(C_1,C_2),\\
I5\ &: L(r,v)=C_1\Rightarrow v\text{ does not sign incompatible }C_2,\\
I6\ &: Conflict(C_1,C_2)\Rightarrow ConflictEvidence(C_1,C_2),\\
I7\ &: Abandon(C)\Rightarrow AbandonProof(C),\\
I8\ &: Execute(C,s)=s'\text{ is deterministic for honest validators.}
\end{align*}
I1--I3 bind application and state seals to closure data. I4 and I5 are the safety boundary. I6 and I7 state accountability for negative outcomes. I8 is restricted to the supported deterministic instruction kernel, not an arbitrary smart-contract language.

\subsection{Quorum model and closure exclusivity}
In Model A, let $N\geq3f+1$ and let each valid closure quorum have cardinality at least $2f+1$. For any two quorums $Q_1,Q_2$,
\begin{equation}
|Q_1\cap Q_2|\geq |Q_1|+|Q_2|-N\geq4f+2-(3f+1)=f+1.
\end{equation}
At most $f$ validators are Byzantine, so $Q_1\cap Q_2$ contains an honest validator.

\textbf{Theorem 1 (Closure Exclusivity).} Fix one resource, predecessor context, epoch, and attempt domain. Under Model A, honest non-equivocation, and the predicate $\mathsf{VCP}$ above, there do not exist incompatible Capsules $C_1,C_2$ and proofs $\Pi_1,\Pi_2$ such that $\mathsf{VCP}(C_1,\Pi_1)$ and $\mathsf{VCP}(C_2,\Pi_2)$ both hold.

\emph{Proof.} Suppose, for contradiction, that such $C_1,C_2,\Pi_1,\Pi_2$ exist. Let $Q_i=\{r.\mathit{witness\_id}:r\in U(\Pi_i)\}$. The threshold clause in $\mathsf{VCP}$ and Model A give $|Q_i|\geq2f+1$. The intersection inequality therefore gives an honest validator $h\in Q_1\cap Q_2$. By the receipt-validity clause of $\mathsf{VCP}$, $h$ issued a valid ACCEPT receipt for each Capsule. Because $C_1$ and $C_2$ are incompatible in the same first-claim context, this contradicts honest non-equivocation. Hence no two incompatible proofs can simultaneously satisfy $\mathsf{VCP}$. Since registration additionally rejects a predecessor context with recorded ConflictEvidence, two incompatible Closed observations are also impossible under these premises. $\square$

The theorem is conditional and safety-only. It does not prove that any Capsule will obtain a valid proof, that a valid proof will be delivered to every observer, or that a pending observation will eventually become terminal. It also does not prove cryptographic key security, permissionless Sybil resistance, economic incentive compatibility, or correctness under arbitrary reconfiguration. In Model B, if $w(Q_i)>2W/3$ and Byzantine weight is at most $W/3$, then $w(Q_1\cap Q_2)>W/3$ and the intersection contains positive honest weight. The executable \texttt{mosaic/safety.py} and model checker validate finite instances of these premises; they do not replace the theorem or establish an unbounded formal proof.

\section{Implementation}
The prototype is written for Python 3.11 or newer. The public repository contains a \texttt{mosaic} namespace for the protocol and permissionless layers and a \texttt{ccd\_nexus} namespace for shared cryptographic primitives and legacy support. The code uses canonical serialization, Ed25519 signatures, digest-bound identifiers, and typed dataclasses for protocol objects.

\subsection{Networking and storage}
The network layer uses asyncio TCP with a four-byte length prefix and JSON payload framing. It supports optional mutual TLS, replay protection, per-peer token buckets, connection caps, frame-size limits, and frame timeouts. Invalid or oversized frames are classified as malformed protocol input; unexpected structural exceptions remain daemon errors. The leaderless dissemination layer does not require a single permanent coordinator for every Capsule.

Durability uses SQLite WAL with full synchronous mode, schema migration, snapshots, and restore of seals, Capsules, receipts, and closures. The long-run harness exposed a race in which a restarted node could receive a Capsule before predecessor seals had completed restoration. The corrected path keeps the Capsule pending or records a retryable protocol rejection. The recovery artifact now reports zero unexpected errors in the v5 local run.

\subsection{Settlement and randomness}
Membership includes signed admission requests, stake bonds, weighted committee selection, withdrawal delay, and slash evidence. SettlementLedger persists bonds, unbonding, rewards, penalties, and fees. This is an executable accounting component, not an externally enforced public economy. The randomness layer uses commit--reveal rounds with fallback and reward/penalty settlement. It provides an operational mechanism for non-reveal handling, not a proof of unbiased randomness or incentive compatibility against all strategic behavior.

\subsection{Availability and execution}
The availability layer uses Reed--Solomon coding over GF(256) with data and parity shards \cite{Reed1960}. It exposes shard storage, sampling, repair, and WAL persistence. The availability network has PUT, FETCH, SAMPLE, and REPAIR paths, but the current evidence is local and does not establish a globally deployed availability network.

The execution kernel supports deterministic SET, DELETE, and ADD\_INT-style instructions with origin, nonce, gas, and state-root binding. Execution is atomic and returns a receipt containing pre- and post-state roots. The kernel is a vehicle for studying binding between protocol closure and execution; it is not a general smart-contract VM.

The TLA+ model names the following properties: \texttt{I1\_NoApplyWithoutClosure}, \texttt{I2\_PredecessorBinding}, \texttt{I3\_SealBinding}, \texttt{I4\_NoTwoIncompatibleClosures}, \texttt{I5\_FirstClaimNonEquivocation}, \texttt{I6\_ConflictEvidencePreserved}, \texttt{I7\_AbandonEvidencePreserved}, and \texttt{I8\_DeterministicExecution}. \section{Experimental Evaluation}
\subsection{Methodology and scope}
The evaluation uses two complementary harnesses. The first is an in-process serialized workload that executes real Capsules, receipts, closures, seals, and hash-chained event records. It compares 1,000 disjoint transitions against 1,000 same-resource competing-claims iterations, evaluates BundleClosure batch sizes 1, 10, and 100 on independent resources, and runs a 120-operation disjoint scaling sweep at 4, 7, 10, and 16 validator processes. The second is the seven-process long-run daemon rehearsal with two Byzantine test identities, a scheduled kill/restart, and partial-frame probes. Neither harness is a WAN experiment.

\subsection{Disjoint, contended, and batching workloads}
The disjoint workload creates 1,000 independent resources and closes one transition on each. The same-resource competing-claims workload creates two distinct Capsules from the same predecessor before either claim is closed. The first claim obtains a ClosureProof and successor StateSeal; the second claim is submitted to the same first-claim context and must be rejected with ConflictEvidence. Each of the 1,000 iterations therefore has one successful closure, one rejected claim, and one evidence artifact. Both the disjoint and competing workloads use four validator objects and four ACCEPT receipts for the winning closure. The batching workload creates independent closures and applies them through BundleClosure at batch sizes 1, 10, and 100. All event logs verified successfully.

The competing-claims row is not a serial same-resource control: two claims are created before the winner is closed, and the loser follows the first-claim conflict path. Every iteration produced one Closed winner, one rejected loser, and one ConflictEvidence artifact; the event log remained verifiable. The measured evidence payload totaled 635,000 bytes, or 635 bytes per rejected claim, and the complete competing-claims accounting was 4,945.97 bytes per successful closure. This contrast supports the state-transition-centric claim in a limited but direct way: independent transitions avoid conflict evidence, whereas incompatible claims sharing one predecessor produce an explicit, measurable terminal artifact. Batch 100 reduces measured protocol bytes per operation by approximately 19.4\% relative to the disjoint baseline in this implementation and workload, while its p95 remains close to batch 10. These are implementation measurements, not claims of superiority over another ledger or of simultaneous WAN concurrency.

\section{Related Work}
\subsection{Ordered BFT and DAG dissemination}
Practical Byzantine fault tolerance established the classic state-machine replication baseline for signed requests, quorum agreement, and deterministic execution \cite{Castro1999PBFT}. The broader SMR literature treats agreement, execution, recovery, and replica coordination as separable protocol layers \cite{Cachin2010SMRByzantine}; surveys of BFT protocols likewise emphasize the difficulty of composing these layers without changing their failure assumptions \cite{Aublin2015Next700BFT,Distler2021BFTSMR}. Bessani et al. argue that deploying a blockchain requires more than consensus, including execution, membership, storage, and operational mechanisms \cite{Bessani2020ConsensusBeginning}; Wang shows that the security/liveness model of deployed DLT protocols can diverge from the traditional academic BFT model \cite{Wang2022DLTBFT}. HotStuff is a leader-based BFT replication protocol with a partially synchronous safety/liveness model and responsive communication phases \cite{Yin2019HotStuff}. Narwhal and Tusk separate reliable dissemination from ordering and use a DAG-based mempool with BFT consensus \cite{Danezis2022Narwhal}. Mysticeti studies low-latency DAG-based BFT and reports a large-scale evaluation \cite{Babel2025Mysticeti}. MOSAIC does not replace these protocols, claim a lower communication bound, or turn a local run into a liveness theorem. Its proposed object is a transition lifecycle whose evidence is consumed by execution and audit layers.

\subsection{Certificates and restricted ordering}
FastPay demonstrates a high-throughput settlement protocol based on Byzantine consistent broadcast for pre-funded payments \cite{Baudet2020FastPay}. Sui Lutris combines a consensusless agreement path with consensus when transactions risk inconsistent concurrent access \cite{Blackshear2024SuiLutris}; its object model makes versions, owners, and authenticated object references first-class \cite{SuiObjectModel}. Polygraph studies accountability in Byzantine agreement by exposing evidence when disagreement becomes unavoidable \cite{Civit2021Polygraph}. These works are close prior art for selective ordering, local certificates, and accountability. Their certificates or evidence objects answer narrower questions—whether a payment is authorized, whether an object access is safe, or which protocol behavior is attributable. ECTC instead defines the terminal semantic interface for one transition: a positive outcome requires the complete closure-and-successor evidence set, a negative outcome preserves a conflict or abandonment artifact, and the absence of either remains pending. This is not a claim to invent certificates or accountability; it is a claim about the falsifiable lifecycle contract imposed on them.

\subsection{Object-centric state and parallel execution}
Tango investigates distributed data structures over a shared log and demonstrates that object-level state and versioning can be built above replicated logging \cite{Balakrishnan2013Tango}. Block-STM uses optimistic concurrency and deterministic validation to execute transactions in parallel while preserving a preset order \cite{Gelashvili2022BlockSTM}. Process-commutative distributed objects and BFT CRDTs study legal operation traces and the space between conflict-free replication and total-order ledgers \cite{Frey2024BFTCRDT}. These results prevent MOSAIC from claiming first use of object-centric execution, optimistic concurrency, commutativity, or reduced ordering scope. Attested append-only memory also shows how authenticated history can constrain an adversary's later claims \cite{Chun2007AttestedMemory}. ECTC does not add another object model or execution scheduler. Its distinction is evidence semantics: a transition's terminal status is Closed only with a valid closure proof and successor seal, while Conflict and Abandoned remain independently inspectable artifacts and Pending is a defined non-terminal state.

\subsection{Novelty boundary and composition claim}
The novelty claim is deliberately narrower than a claim of inventing a new cryptographic primitive, quorum rule, object model, certificate format, scheduler, or BFT protocol. ECTC is a semantic interface over those mechanisms. A system satisfies the proposed interface only when it can distinguish four observational states—Closed, Conflict, Abandoned, and Pending—and can validate the artifact predicate associated with each state. The positive path is conjunctive: a valid, identifier-bound, threshold-supported ClosureProof is insufficient for Closed until the matching successor StateSeal is also observed. The negative paths are not discarded error codes: incompatible claims and explicit abandonment remain evidence-bearing terminal states. Pending is not failure and is not success.

This boundary answers the composition objection in a falsifiable way. If the lifecycle is removed, a prototype can still use objects, certificates, optimistic execution, or quorum voting, but it no longer satisfies ECTC's outcome classifier, the VCP-based Closure Exclusivity theorem, or the evidence-preservation invariants I1--I8. If the same primitives are retained but a system silently treats a missing proof or successor seal as success, it fails the proposed semantics. The contribution is therefore the specified conjunction, its safety argument, its explicit non-implication of liveness, and its executable tests—not priority over the individual components.

\section{Security Analysis}
\subsection{Threat model}
The prototype assumes unforgeable Ed25519 signatures, deterministic canonical serialization, a bounded Byzantine set or stake weight, and honest non-equivocation within the modeled first-claim context. It considers malformed messages, replay, stale predecessors, conflicting Capsules, delayed receipts, validator restart, beacon non-reveal, and local shard loss. It does not claim protection against compromised private keys, compromised hosts, colluding stake majorities, or all network-layer denial-of-service strategies.

\subsection{Safety}
The ECTC invariants and Theorem 1 provide the stated safety boundary. The implementation rejects application without a valid ClosureProof, verifies predecessor and successor bindings, rejects incompatible closures with recorded evidence, and checks execution receipts against successor roots. The TLA+ model and Python checker are bounded/model-checked support, not an unbounded proof of the entire implementation.

\subsection{Evidence completeness versus liveness}
Evidence completeness is a predicate over an observed protocol state: at time $t$, a Capsule is complete when a verifier can validate exactly one of the Closed, Conflict, or Abandoned artifact sets. Liveness is a temporal property. For an initial observation time $t_0$, define
\begin{equation}
\mathsf{Live}(C,t_0)\Longleftrightarrow\exists t\geq t_0:\mathsf{Closed}_t(C)\lor\mathsf{Conflict}_t(C)\lor\mathsf{Abandoned}_t(C).
\end{equation}
The implication $\mathsf{EvidenceComplete}_t(C)\Rightarrow\mathsf{Live}(C,t_0)$ is invalid in general: evidence may already be complete because a conflicting or abandonment artifact was produced, while ECTC itself says nothing about whether a pending Capsule will ever reach any terminal outcome. Conversely, a liveness theorem would require a separate network, scheduling, membership, and failure model; it cannot be inferred from the ECTC classifier or Theorem 1.

The local long-run achieved 120/120 completed operations and liveness ratio 1.0 under the declared schedule. This is an operational observation of one implementation and harness, not a theorem of eventual termination. The beacon fallback preserves progress when reveal weight is low in the local model, but these observations do not establish liveness under arbitrary WAN partitions, operator churn, public admission, or adversarial stake concentration.

\subsection{Accountability}
ConflictEvidence and AbandonProof make negative protocol outcomes inspectable. The hash-chained event log makes operational incidents and upgrades tamper-evident within one log. These artifacts provide evidence for review; they do not by themselves prove that every external observer saw the same message schedule or that a Byzantine operator will accept a slashing decision.

\subsection{Replay and equivocation}
Capsules and receipts bind identifiers, epochs, attempts, predecessor identifiers, signatures, and status. ReplayGuard rejects duplicate message identifiers and the protocol rejects old-epoch receipts. First-claim locks preserve local non-equivocation; quorum intersection supplies the conditional closure-exclusivity argument.

\subsection{Crash recovery}
SQLite WAL, snapshots, and restore paths recover seals, Capsules, receipts, and closures. The long-run correction ensures that a Capsule received before predecessor restoration enters pending/retry handling rather than being counted as an unexpected error. The evidence is local and does not cover correlated storage loss or operator key recovery.

\subsection{Availability}
Reed--Solomon encoding and repair are implemented and tested, and the availability network exposes sampling and repair paths. No claim is made that local shard repair proves a production data-availability layer. A public deployment would need independent providers, retention policies, sampling schedules, withholding tests, and correlated-failure experiments.

\subsection{Permissionless limitations}
MOSAIC is not yet a public permissionless network. The settlement and admission code is an implementation component; permissionless security would require independent operators, Sybil resistance, stake-distribution analysis, enforceable slashing, governance, key isolation, public genesis, and WAN experiments. Economic security, unbiased randomness, and public-token security are not established here.

\section{Limitations}
The experiments are local and serialized or process-local. The validator scaling sweep increases receipt fan-out but does not model WAN latency, packet loss, heterogeneous hardware, clock drift, NAT, or independent failure domains. The long-run uses seven processes on one host and deterministic Byzantine identities; it is not a public testnet. The execution kernel is intentionally small, the weighted theorem extension is stated under explicit assumptions, and the TLA+ model is bounded. The byte accounting reports JSON and event-log costs, which are implementation-dependent. The paper does not claim that ECTC is globally novel relative to all object-centric, certificate-based, CRDT, or BFT literature; it presents a falsifiable composition and evidence hypothesis.

\section{Discussion}
The scientific value of MOSAIC, if supported by independent replication, would be a change in the object whose integrity is made explicit. A conventional ledger can retain transaction data, votes, and state roots, but a protocol implementation may still expose a rejected or abandoned claim only as an absence from the committed path. ECTC makes the negative paths part of the claimed lifecycle. That may matter in settlement networks, registries, multi-party workflows, or systems where the reason for rejection is itself subject to audit.

The cost is visible. The 1,000-operation decomposition shows that four signed receipts, closure records, JSON framing, and hash-chained events dominate the measured bytes. Batching reduces bytes per operation in this implementation, but batching is appropriate only for compatible independent closures. A future binary codec or proof aggregation could reduce representation overhead, but any optimization must preserve the evidence that supports I6 and I7.

The closest future comparison is workload-by-workload rather than “MOSAIC versus blockchain” in the abstract. Disjoint resources should be evaluated for ordering scope and closure cost; simultaneous conflicting resources should be evaluated for detection latency and evidence completeness; shared state should be evaluated for closure contention and recovery; availability should be evaluated for provider overhead and correlated shard loss; and permissionless deployment should be evaluated for admission, stake concentration, slashing, and churn. The current artifacts establish the measurement machinery but do not yet supply the independent-WAN evidence for those claims.

\section{Conclusion}
This paper introduced MOSAIC and its central semantics, Evidence-Complete Transition Closure. Every observed terminal transition outcome is intended to be Closed with a ClosureProof and successor StateSeal, Conflict with ConflictEvidence, or Abandoned with AbandonProof; pending is explicitly non-terminal. The paper stated the unweighted quorum assumptions for Closure Exclusivity, described a weighted extension, mapped I1--I8 to TLA+, added a 20-case adversarial matrix, and evaluated disjoint, competing-claims, batched, scaled, and crash/restart workloads. The competing-claims experiment closed 1,000 winners, rejected 1,000 losing claims, and generated 1,000 ConflictEvidence artifacts.

The implementation and local artifacts support a coherent protocol hypothesis. They do not establish a public permissionless testnet, WAN liveness, economic security, an unbounded formal proof, or universal performance superiority. Independent WAN deployment with operator-controlled keys, stronger formal checking of epochs and weighted membership, and fair comparisons with HotStuff, Narwhal/Tusk, Sui Lutris, FastPay, Mysticeti, Block-STM, and BFT-object systems are outside the present evaluation scope. Within the reported scope, the defensible claim is that MOSAIC is a reproducible protocol and systems artifact whose distinctive hypothesis is an evidence-complete state-transition closure lifecycle.
